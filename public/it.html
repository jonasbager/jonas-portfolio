// Philosophy

How I run IT

Four things that shape every decision I make about infrastructure, tools, and security.

🔓

Enable, don't gatekeep

The best IT setup is one people barely notice - because everything just works and the right tools are available. I'm not the person who defaults to no. I find the solution that's both secure and actually usable.

🔧

Hands-on by choice

I don't consider the physical or technical work beneath the role. Getting into the weeds of a Fortigate config, sorting a misbehaving server, pulling cable under a deadline - that's part of the job, and honestly part of why I like it.

🎯

Build for how people actually work

Not how the org chart says they should. I talk to the people who'll use the tools before I design the setup. The best IT environments I've built have always started from understanding real workflows.

✅

Start from yes

Requests are opportunities, not risks to block by default. Security comes from good controls - conditional access, least privilege, audit logs - not from saying no. Almost every "no" I've inherited has had a better answer.

// Built, not just talked about

Things I've actually shipped

AI in IT stops being interesting when it's theoretical. Here's what I've built and put into production.

Multi-user AI agent

Slack AI agent on on-prem GPU

ProblemTeams wanted a shared AI assistant in Slack, but couldn't use a cloud-only tool - data isolation between users was non-negotiable.
What I builtSelf-hosted agent running on an on-prem GPU server. Strict session isolation so one user's mail, files and HR data never reaches another. Admin can maintain it without reading sensitive data. Keys held by the IT lead.
Why it mattersThe team got a genuinely useful AI tool without accepting a "share everything" architecture. Governance baked in from day one.
Enterprise integration

MCP server for company data

ProblemAI assistants (Claude, Copilot, ChatGPT) couldn't securely query internal company data without opening up everything.
What I builtAn MCP server that lets AI assistants query company data via a controlled interface, secured with OAuth against IdentityServer and deployed in Microsoft 365 via Copilot Studio.
Why it mattersAny approved AI assistant can now answer questions about internal data - with proper auth, audit logs and no raw database access.
AI support tooling

AI bot on top of Jira

ProblemSupport tickets were handled inconsistently - triage took too long, the same questions kept arriving, and backlog grooming was manual.
What I builtA bot that ingests tickets, categorises and triages them, and drafts first responses. Production readiness covered PII exposure, failure modes, stability under load and bus factor.
Why it mattersFaster triage, less repetitive work for the team - and no one person is the single point of failure for the whole thing.

// Why this is unusual

A background most IT people don't have

Product thinking inside an IT role

Most IT professionals haven't spent years in product management. I have. That means I approach every tool choice, onboarding flow, and security policy with the same user-centred thinking I'd apply to building a product — because that's exactly what they are. Products your colleagues use every day.

When you've worked in product, you develop a specific kind of empathy for how people actually use tools — not how they're supposed to, but how they really do. You learn to spot the workflow that's quietly costing people 20 minutes a day. You get comfortable talking to non-technical stakeholders about technical decisions. And you develop a healthy intolerance for unnecessary complexity.

One more thing that doesn't fit neatly elsewhere: I've served as an IT expert arbitrator in legal disputes a handful of times. It requires deep technical knowledge, clear communication under pressure, and the ability to explain complex systems to people who aren't technical. Not something many IT professionals can say.
